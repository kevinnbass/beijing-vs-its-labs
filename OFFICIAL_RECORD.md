## 1. What the documents establish, in six sentences

1. Since 2016 the Party's leader has said in published speeches that core technology controlled by others is the greatest hidden danger and that home-made products "must be used"; in 2025 the Central Committee wrote "extraordinary measures" for integrated circuits into the five-year plan.
2. That line is in binding and planning documents: Party and government organs at township level and above, and the public institutions that support them, must when buying desktop and portable computers and general-purpose servers require that the CPU and the operating system pass the security-and-reliability evaluation (joint notices of the finance and industry ministries), and the published lists of processors (2023) and of AI chips (2026) that passed name only Chinese makers (applying is voluntary and open only to entities registered in China); products made in China get a 20% price preference in government purchasing; provinces and cities set targets for the share of home-made chips in new computing centres (Jiangsu 70% or more by 2030 and 50% by 2025, both marked indicative; Sichuan and Guangdong aim for over 50% and 70%; Shanghai over 50% by 2025 and over 70% independently controllable; Tianjin over 60% by 2026; Hebei 60% or more of software and hardware; Inner Mongolia 50% or more; Shandong 90% in a newspaper's account). Two limits, both in the documents: every binding purchase rule read is addressed to state bodies or to public money, and in each of the three purchase notices the same sentence adds that other units may leave this requirement out; and every home-made percentage found is provincial or municipal, none national.
3. Regulators acted against Nvidia by name: the market regulator announced on Dec 9, 2024 that it had recently opened an antitrust case and the cyberspace regulator summoned the company over the H20 (Jul 31, 2025).
4. Official Chinese documents also show what the companies used and wanted: a Shanghai government reply says that among the city's computing suppliers internet companies' own clusters run mainly on Nvidia cards while the three big operators mainly buy Huawei and other home-made clusters; an official of the state planner's policy research office said at its press conference that it is guiding home-made large models to do more to adapt to home-made chips; a Party-journal investigation says migration is costly and slow.
5. Two companies say in filings that the state stopped purchases: Nvidia ("restricted by the PRC government") and Alibaba ("requested Chinese technology companies to halt purchases of certain chips"). The US Commerce Secretary told the Senate the same on Apr 22, 2026, as his "understanding": the cloud companies "want to buy these chips" and the "Chinese central government has not let them as of yet".
6. The same body of documents holds the other side: every leadership text keeps an "open door" clause; ministers welcomed Nvidia by name; the commerce ministry attacks US controls and smooths the way for Chinese companies' computing centres abroad; local vouchers pay for rented computing that is not home-made too (of 19 rules counted, 14 at one rate for all and 4 at a lower rate; one, Sichuan's, pays for home-made computing only); two universities publish notices of Nvidia hardware, holding suppliers to the government procurement law's conditions; the 20% preference goes by where a product is made, with foreign-funded firms treated equally; and Baidu and Alibaba tell investors that their own chips are in use or in scaled production. And US government documents read parts of Beijing's conduct, the antitrust case above all, as leverage or retaliation in trade talks, while the Trade Representative calls the purchase decision China's own (section 4).

**What follows.** On documents alone, the claim "the state's declared policy pulled against what its AI companies were using and buying" is established. The claim "Beijing forbade the purchases" is established only through the two company filings and the US officials in section 4; Beijing itself has published no such instruction and its spokesmen decline to confirm one (section 5).

## 2. Chinese party-state documents: the push toward home-made chips

- Xi Jinping, forum on cybersecurity and informatization, Apr 19, 2016: "核心技术受制于人是我们最大的隐患" (core technology being controlled by others is our greatest hidden danger); and of home-made products, "该用的就要用" (what should be used must be used) [cn-xi-20160419]. The same speech speaks of internet companies by kind: "一个互联网企业即便规模再大、市值再高,如果核心元器件严重依赖外国,供应链的“命门”掌握在别人手里,那就好比在别人的墙基上砌房子,再大再漂亮也可能经不起风雨,甚至会不堪一击。" (however large an internet company and however high its market value, if it depends heavily on foreign countries for core components and the "vital gate" of its supply chain is in others' hands, it is like building a house on someone else's foundation: however big and handsome, it may not stand wind and rain, and may fall at a single blow) [cn-policy-xi-coretech-2016]. ### 2.4 Regulators' acts against foreign chips

- Market regulator, notice dated Dec 9, 2024 ("发布时间：2024-12-09 19:30" [cn-policy-samr-open-original]), saying it had recently (近日) opened the case: "因英伟达公司涉嫌违反《中华人民共和国反垄断法》... 市场监管总局依法对英伟达公司开展立案调查" (because Nvidia is suspected of violating the Anti-Monopoly Law ... the regulator has opened an investigation of Nvidia) [cn-policy-samr-open-original].
- Market regulator, Sept 15, 2025: "经初步调查,英伟达公司违反《中华人民共和国反垄断法》和《市场监管总局关于附加限制性条件批准英伟达公司收购迈络思科技有限公司股权案反垄断审查决定的公告》,市场监管总局依法决定对其实施进一步调查" (after a preliminary investigation, Nvidia violated the Anti-Monopoly Law and the regulator's decision approving its purchase of Mellanox with conditions; the regulator has decided to investigate further) [cn-policy-samr-further-original].
- Cyberspace regulator, Jul 31, 2025: it "约谈了英伟达公司,要求英伟达公司就对华销售的H20算力芯片漏洞后门安全风险问题进行说明并提交相关证明材料" (summoned Nvidia and required it to explain the security risks of vulnerabilities and backdoors in the H20 chips sold to China and to submit supporting material) [cn-policy-cac-h20-original].
- The precedent: the Cybersecurity Review Office on Micron, published on the cyberspace regulator's site on May 21, 2023 (the office sits in that regulator: "网络安全审查办公室设在国家互联网信息办公室" [cn-policy-review-measures]; corrected Nov 23, 2026: this line had named the regulator itself as the issuer): its products "存在较严重网络安全问题隐患,对我国关键信息基础设施供应链造成重大安全风险" (carry fairly serious hidden cybersecurity problems and pose a major security risk to the supply chain of China's critical information infrastructure) [cn-policy-micron-decision]. Its operative sentence: "我国内关键信息基础设施的运营者应停止采购美光公司产品" (operators of critical information infrastructure in China should stop buying Micron's products). The page calls it the review office's conclusion that the products do not pass the review: "网络安全审查办公室依法作出不予通过网络安全审查的结论" [cn-policy-micron-decision]. So when the review office barred a foreign chip maker's products for a class of buyers, the conclusion was published. For Nvidia the pages read hold a summons and no decision. That cuts both ways: Beijing can publish a ban and has not; or its method for Nvidia was unpublished guidance, as the two company filings say.
- Customs Law, article 24: "国家限制进出口的货物,没有进出口许可证件的,不予放行" (goods whose import or export the state restricts shall not be released without licence documents); article 28: "进出口货物应当接受海关查验" (import and export goods shall be subject to customs inspection) [cn-policy-customs-law-wayback, a finance-ministry page in an Internet Archive capture of Apr 10, 2026]. Customs may inspect anything and must hold restricted goods that lack a licence. None of the pages read is a list of restricted imports that names these chips, so the reported customs holds (Tier 2) rest either on the general inspection power or on something unpublished.

## 7. What changes in the argument when it rests on documents

1. **Stronger.** The claim no longer needs unnamed sources. The Shanghai reply, the state planner's statement, the purchasing rules and the two filings carry it; so does the US Commerce Secretary's testimony of Apr 22, 2026. The full reading of the policy pages adds Xi's 2016 sentence on internet companies that depend on foreign core components, the 2014 clause "encouraging" internet companies to buy secure-and-reliable systems, the cyberspace regulator's 2026 plan telling its companies to develop high-end AI chips, and an official evaluation list of AI chips with only Chinese makers on it.
2. **Narrower.** The documents support "declared policy pulled against the companies' practice, and regulators stopped certain purchases". They do not support "China will not give import permits" as a published fact: no permit rule for these chips was found, and the one company inquiry found asks whether a permit is even required. The full reading sharpens this. Every binding purchase rule read is addressed to state bodies or to public money, and says other units need not apply it. Every home-made percentage found is provincial or municipal. The measures Beijing has published in the chip dispute are export controls of its own. When it barred Micron's products it published the decision; for Nvidia it has published a summons and two investigation notices.
3. **More two-sided.** The best evidence against the position is also official: a 2020 regulatory decision that obliges Nvidia to keep supplying the Chinese market, ministers welcoming Nvidia by name, vouchers that pay for foreign chips, facilitation of computing abroad, and universities buying Nvidia hardware in published notices. Added by the full reading: Xi's own "not behind closed doors" and "can be brought in but must be secure and controllable"; equal treatment of foreign-funded firms in the 20% preference; the vice minister's "the roadmap does not instruct companies"; and Baidu's and Alibaba's statements that their own chips are in use, which point to reasons of their own for moving off Nvidia.
4. **Xi.** Herzinger's point is fully documentary, and so is its limit: Xi's texts set the aim and say home-made products must be used; none decides the Nvidia question.
5. **What Beijing meant.** Several American documents read parts of Beijing's conduct as leverage or retaliation in trade talks: the Trade Representative's 2026 estimate, the US-China commission's 2025 report, CRS, the White House's fact sheet of Nov 1, 2025 and Senator Kaine (section 4). The Trade Representative in May and Dec 2025 and May 2026 called the H200 not negotiated and China's decision «sovereign». The regulator's Party group framed the case as a political task (2.6). Dates of the acts sit near the talks (7 of 13 restrictive acts within 7 days of an in-person meeting, against 26% of days; tables/talks_and_acts.tsv), but dates cannot show motive. The direction of the policy stands under either reading.
