# Did Beijing's chip policy pull against China's own AI companies?

*Yes, on direction, with five limits: four on how, and one on what Beijing meant by it. Every statement below carries a numbered reference to a saved source.*

Written Nov 23, 2026 (UTC). Made public the same day. The figure that goes with it is in the same folder: figure/beijing_vs_labs.html.

Written by Claude, Anthropic's model. Anthropic has argued in public for controls on chip exports to China (that is from my general knowledge; no saved page is cited for it), so judge this note by its sources.

## The claim tested

Palmer Luckey, in a clip posted on Nov 19, 2026: "China doesn't want NVIDIA to sell GPUs to Chinese companies." [1] And next: "there are Chinese companies that want to buy Nvidia GPUs" [2]. The first sentence is from a machine transcript of the clip (0:11 to 0:16). The second is from the text of the post that carries the clip; the post begins it with the word Like, and so does a second machine transcript. A published transcript of the whole session, at the DealBook Summit 2026, has both sentences in the same words, but ends the first with two more words, like this: "Chinese companies like this. There are Chinese companies that want to buy Nvidia GPUs" [3]. That page does not say how its transcript was made. He goes on, by the machine transcript (0:18 to 0:36): "There's 600,000 H200 GPUs sitting in a port right now", because "China will not give them an import permit", "because they want to build an internal domestic processor industry, silicon industry, not build one that is dependent on the United States" [1]. Nobody has checked any of this by ear.

**The answer.** From 2024 to 2026 the Chinese state's declared policy, and what its regulators did, pulled China's AI companies away from Nvidia chips. The companies went on using those chips, ordering them and asking for them. Five limits apply. First, Beijing was not one actor: its security and planning side pressed against the chips, its commerce and foreign-affairs side was more open to them. Second, the pressure came as public warnings and unpublished guidance, not as a published ban, and the seven state-media items found that only warn about Nvidia's chips all fall between Aug 1 and Sept 24, 2025; three later ones mix caution with openness to the H200. Third, some purchases were approved and some chips came in. Fourth, the companies had reasons of their own, and American curbs and conditions pushed too: Nvidia's reports since November 2025 say "we are already effectively foreclosed from the China market by U.S. export controls" [4], and of the 3,444,160 chips in the licence applications that Washington's summary of May 11, 2026 records, 1,888,950 (55%; the sum is mine) were in applications it returned without action; its table gives no reason, and the committee that released it says the summary does not show every application in full [5] [6]. Fifth, what Beijing meant by it is not settled: the US-China commission saw in the market regulator's move against Nvidia, which came as the regulator dropped an antitrust probe into Google, "China's willingness to both exert leverage and ease pressure as a negotiating tactic" [7], while the Trade Representative, asked whether China would let its companies buy the H200, said it was "gonna be a sovereign decision for China" (captions, not checked by ear, at 5:59 in the recording) [8]. The direction of the policy stands under either reading.

**How to read the references.** Each number leads to one saved source in the list at the end. A filled number is a document, a filing, a named person on the record, or a published commentary of state media or an industry body. An open number is a press report that cites unnamed people, a research firm's count, or a machine transcript; machine captions of a broadcast are marked as such, with the time in the recording, and nobody has checked them by ear. Words between quotation marks were found in the saved copy by a script. English in round brackets after Chinese or Japanese is my rendering, not an official translation.

## 1. The state's declared line: use home-made chips

**1.** **The leader.** Xi Jinping said in 2016 that the internet's core technology is China's greatest weak point and that having core technology controlled by others is its greatest hidden danger: "互联网核心技术是我们最大的“命门”，核心技术受制于人是我们最大的隐患" [9]. In the same speech he said that an internet company, however large, whose core components depend heavily on foreign countries is like a house built on someone else's foundation: "那就好比在别人的墙基上砌房子" [10]. In April 2025 he told the Politburo to concentrate forces on high-end chips: "集中力量攻克高端芯片、基础软件等核心技术" [11]. In October 2025 the Central Committee's recommendations for the next five-year plan called for extraordinary measures, "采取超常规措施", in integrated circuits and other fields [12]. A search of the People's Daily database of his speeches and activities for Nvidia, its chief executive's name, the H20 and the H200 found nothing, while the same search finds Qualcomm, Microsoft and Intel in his texts; the database lacks at least one of his meetings of the period, so this is a strong negative, not a complete one (the searches, with their controls, are in tables/xi_searches.tsv).

**2.** **The rules.** Notices of the finance and industry ministries, published on Dec 26, 2023, require Party and government bodies at township level and above, and the public institutions attached to them, to write into their purchase requirements for desktop computers and servers that the processor and the operating system meet the security-and-reliability evaluation: "应当将CPU、操作系统符合安全可靠测评要求纳入采购需求" [13] [14]. The two evaluation centres' results of May 26, 2026, on one centre's own site, list nine AI training and inference chips that passed, every one from a Chinese maker [15]; their results of Sept 20, 2026 add two more, HiSilicon's Ascend 950 and Kunlunxin's M100 [16]. Firms apply of their own accord, and firms and users choose whether to use the results: "测评结果由企业和用户自主选择使用" [17]. Home-made products get a 20% price preference in government purchasing: "对本国产品的报价给予20%的价格扣除" [18]. The test is where a product is made, not who owns its maker: state, private and foreign-funded firms "平等享受对本国产品的政府采购支持政策" (enjoy the purchasing support for home-made products equally), and regions and departments may not, in government purchasing, name a brand or restrict where a brand is registered or who owns it [18].

**3.** **The targets, and the money.** Eight of China's 31 provincial-level governments were found to set a share for home-made computing hardware; the table gives each in its document's own words. Most are aims, several with the word 力争 (strive to); Jiangsu's own table marks its figures as indicative, not binding [19]. Of the other 23, 21 have a plan that was read and sets no share (Chongqing's plan of 2026 sets a share only for green power [20]). Beijing city's plan aims, by 2027, at a home-made stack of hardware and software and "具备100%自主可控智算中心建设能力" (the ability to build computing centres that are 100% independently controllable) [21]; Guizhou's aims at a national rank, "国产智算能力位居全国前列" [22]; Shaanxi's computing item sets no share; on building, it says it will "按需建设算力基础设施" (build computing infrastructure as needed) [23]. Tibet's plan is known only from a trade newspaper's account (in the table), and for Xinjiang no computing plan was found. The 31 rows are in tables/provinces_31.tsv, beside this note.

**4.** **The agencies and the state's media, in public.** The Ministry of State Security warned in July 2025 that some chips, smart devices and software made abroad may have had backdoors planted on purpose when they were designed and made: "一些境外生产的芯片、智能设备或者软件可能在设计制造阶段就被故意预埋了“后门”" [44]. In May 2026 Li Chao, of the state planner's policy research office, said at its press conference that it was guiding home-made large models to do more to adapt to home-made computing chips: "指导国产大模型加大力度适配国产算力芯片" [45]. State media went further and named the chip. After the cyberspace regulator summoned Nvidia, a People's Daily commentary of Aug 1, 2025 said that 'sick' chips must never be allowed on duty: "绝不能让“带病”的芯片上岗" [46]. Yuyuan Tantian, an account of the state broadcaster [47], wrote on Aug 10, 2025 that the H20 was neither green, nor advanced, nor safe, and: "作为消费者，我们当然可以选择，不买" (as consumers we can of course choose not to buy) [48]. Of 42 state and Party media items on Nvidia chips found from July 2025 to November 2026, the seven that only warn about them all date from Aug 1 to Sept 24, 2025 [46] [49]. No later item found is a warning alone; three, of Dec 13 and 14, 2025 and Jan 7, 2026, mix caution with openness to the H200, the Economic Daily piece quoted below among them (the count and the reading of each item are in tables/state_media_items.tsv). An industry body had urged caution about American chips in general on Dec 3, 2024: the Internet Society of China called on Chinese companies to be prudent in choosing to buy them, "审慎选择采购美国芯片", giving Washington's export controls as its ground, and in the same sentence to use chips made in China by domestic and foreign-funded firms alike, "积极使用内外资企业在华生产制造的芯片" [50]. When Washington cleared the H200, a signed commentary in the Economic Daily said that Chinese users might buy it selectively but would not again pay for the whole lot, "中国用户可能会选择性购买H200芯片，但不会再全盘埋单", and foresaw a market on two tracks, "“多元采购+自主为主”的双轨模式" (buying from many sources, with home-made first) [51].

## 2. What the companies used and wanted: Nvidia

**5.** **A Shanghai government body says so.** Shanghai's Commission of Economy and Informatization wrote, in a reply dated Apr 30, 2025 and published on its site on Nov 10, 2025, that in the city the internet companies' own computing clusters, which serve market demand, run mainly on Nvidia high-performance cards, "互联网企业自建算力集群，满足市场化企业需求，主要以英伟达高性能卡为主", while the three big telecom operators mainly buy Huawei and other home-made clusters, "三大运营商主要购置华为等国产化智算集群", to diversify the city's computing [52].

**6.** **The Party's journal, a chip maker and a model maker say why.** An investigation by a joint study group of the Party journal Qiushi's economics desk and the research institute CCID, carried by the National Data Administration in May 2026, found that moving off Nvidia's software is costly and slow: "迁移成本高昂、适配周期漫长" [53]. A Chinese maker of AI chips, Enflame, says in its final prospectus of Sept 8, 2026 that international makers led by Nvidia are ahead of home-made ones in hardware, software and systems, "均领先于国产厂商" [54]. Its reply to the exchange's listing inquiry says that at the start of the period its accounts cover, when home-made AI chips were not yet mature, internet companies bought mainly international products for commercial reasons: "出于商业化考量，互联网行业客户采购主要以国际产品为主"; and that after the state's East Data, West Computing hub project began at the end of 2022, computing-centre projects became one of the main early sources of demand for home-made AI chips: "智算中心项目随之成为国产AI芯片初期主要的需求场景之一" [55]. A model maker says the same of its rivals. iFlytek's record of its results meeting of Aug 21, 2026: other model makers commonly use Nvidia chips two generations ahead in performance, with a better ecosystem and more efficient training, "其他模型厂商普遍使用性能领先两代的英伟达芯片，其生态更完善，各类算法的训练与移植效率更高". iFlytek itself is on the US Entity List, the same record says [56]. Its report for the first half of 2026 says home-made computing "对比英伟达中端芯片H200尚存在明显差距" (still falls clearly short of Nvidia's mid-range H200 chip), though it says a model was trained wholly on it in April [57].

**7.** **The orders** (press reports citing unnamed people, then two exchange notices). In the first three months of 2025, Chinese companies including ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent "have placed at least $16 billion in orders for" Nvidia's H20 chips, The Information reported, by two people, as Reuters relayed it [58]. Reuters, Dec 31, 2025, by two people: Chinese technology companies "have placed orders for more than 2 million H200 chips for 2026" [59]. Reuters reported on Oct 20, 2026 that ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent "have ordered more than 3 million of" Nvidia's RTX PRO 5500 cards, priced together at over $35 billion, "in a rush for a less powerful but export-compliant chip" [60]. Two listed Chinese companies announced purchases of cards named PRO 5500 to the Shenzhen exchange, one a week before that report and one after. Tianyang Technology, Oct 13, 2026: "20,000 张具体型号为 NVIDIA RTX PRO 5500 Blackwell 的英伟达 GPU 产品" (20,000 Nvidia GPUs of that model), bought from a seller it calls only Company X, and "区别于大模型训练用 GPU 产品" (unlike GPUs for training large models), mainly for inference, graphics and other professional work [61]. The exchange later found that Tianyang's first report of the purchase, a post on its WeChat account of Oct 9, did not say what the cards were for, and called it "不准确、不完整" (inaccurate, incomplete) [62]. Youzu, for a wholly owned subsidiary, in a notice dated Nov 3, 2026: GPUs of the model PRO5500 (PCIe) for 4,225 million yuan including tax, a price the notice says may change, from a seller it calls only Company A; the notice does not name the maker [63].

**8.** **The pushing back.** The Financial Times, Aug 20, 2025: Chinese regulators had urged more use of home-made chips in recent years, "but tech giants from Alibaba to ByteDance argued that their AI development would be impaired without Nvidia's chips"; the paper adds that, by some close to the companies, "they have become more accepting of a switch, especially for" inference [64]. Reuters's headline of Sept 4, 2025: "Chinese firms still want Nvidia chips despite government pressure not to buy, sources say" [65]. Nori Chiou, an investment director at White Oak Capital Partners, quoted by Reuters in December 2025, said cloud companies were "lobbying the government to relax restrictions on a conditional basis" [66]. On Jul 30, 2026 a company asked the commerce ministry, on its public message board, how to import H200 chips lawfully: "我司计划合规进口H200数据中心GPU" [67]. The ministry's reply of Aug 19, 2026 speaks only of what exporters must do; it does not answer the question about imports. A Chinese trade site, Leiphone, Aug 31, 2025, quoted a salesman it did not name, who was still upbeat about the H20 market: "国内还是有需求的，但至于现在能不能采，什么时候采就不太清楚" (there is still demand at home, but whether one can buy now, and when, is not very clear). It also quoted a manager who had worked closely with Nvidia, under a name the site says is a pseudonym, Zheng Xing: there was hardly any demand for the H20, state firms and other government-led buyers would have none, and "BAT也在响应国家号召，不会再买新的H20" (Baidu, Alibaba and Tencent, too, are answering the state's call and will not buy new H20s) [68]. Hanna Dohmen, a research analyst at Georgetown University's Center for Security and Emerging Technology (CSET), put the general point in her prepared statement for a hearing of the US-China commission, Feb 6, 2025: before the American controls "the Chinese government fought against the strong pull of commercial incentives", and "Chinese consumers preferred to use the highest-performance chips designed by Nvidia and other foreign firms" [69].

**9.** **They went abroad for Nvidia chips, and one lab went back to them for training.** Alibaba and ByteDance "are among the tech groups training their latest large language models (LLMs) in data centres across Southeast Asia" (Financial Times, November 2025); by people the paper cites, "there had been a steady increase in training in offshore locations after the Trump administration moved in April to restrict sales of the H20" [70]. Tencent's president spoke of such capacity on its results call of Nov 18, 2026, asked about the latest American restrictions: "everyone is actually having less compute, advanced compute to work with, no matter when you actually started leasing or building capacities outside of China" [71]. The leasing the paper describes is, by its account, "compliant with US export controls" [70]. Shipping chips to such companies' own names abroad is another matter: in the Commerce Department's table of May 11, 2026, none of the five applications to ship Nvidia chips to consignees in Malaysia, Indonesia and Thailand, such as Bytedance System and Alibaba Cloud, was approved (1,118,500 chips; the sum is mine): all are marked RWA [5], the short form of the department's Bureau of Industry and Security for "Returned without action (RWA)" [72]; the papers do not say why. The Financial Times reported on Dec 20, 2025, by people it cites, that Tencent had the use of advanced Nvidia chips owned by a Japanese firm, Datasection, in a data centre outside Osaka; the firm would not confirm its customer, and Tencent said its use of "cloud computing services is both transparent and legal". The paper calls this "a legal but geopolitically fraught strategy" and gives American curbs as the cause: "China's big tech companies have been forced by US curbs to seek ways of accessing Nvidia's top-tier chips overseas" [73]. Datasection's own filings name only a customer of "世界最大規模のクラウドサービスプロバイダー" (one of the world's largest cloud service providers), for a centre to be opened in Osaka [74]; in January 2026 they put the start of that service back to March 2026, the customer having been served since September 2025 on a partner's GPU servers [75], and a notice of May 7, 2026 says the centre had been moved from Osaka to Inzai, in Chiba [76]. DeepSeek "delayed the release of its new model after failing to train it using Huawei's chips": by three unnamed people it "was encouraged by authorities to adopt Huawei's Ascend processor rather than use Nvidia's systems", met "persistent technical issues", and ended by using "Nvidia chips for training and Huawei's for inference" (Financial Times, Aug 13, 2025) [77]. Another unnamed person gave the paper a second cause of the delay, slow labelling of data. Later claims run the other way, each the company's or a local government's own, and none says how many chips or how long. Zhipu said in January 2026 that its image model had completed "its full training cycle on Chinese-made chips", Huawei's Ascend; the same report adds: "Zhipu did not say how many processors or how long it took to train its model" [78]. Meituan's technical blog calls its model of June 2026 "业界首个在五万卡国产算力集群上完成全流程训练与推理的万亿参数模型" (the first trillion-parameter model trained and served end to end on a home-made cluster of 50,000 cards) [79]. Of DeepSeek's later model V4, Reuters wrote in April 2026: "Huawei said its chips were used in some of the V4's training process" [80]; a rival, iFlytek, told investors days later that "DS V4 的训练仍然是基于英伟达卡进行的" (V4's training was still done on Nvidia cards), a competitor's claim that nobody has checked [81]. In June 2026 a group that includes Huawei said, by the Shenzhen government's account as the trade site Tom's Hardware relays it, that it had completed "full-parameter post-training" of V4-Pro on Ascend 910C chips; the site's own caution: "it doesn't demonstrate that the chips can pre-train a frontier model from scratch", and "DeepSeek itself hasn't commented" [82].

## 3. What Beijing did about it

Points 10 to 12 rest on documents, filings and named officials; points 13 to 16 on press reports citing unnamed people. Point 16 also sets Washington's own table of licence applications, a document, beside those reports.

### On the record

**10.** **Regulators acted against Nvidia by name.** The market regulator announced on Dec 9, 2024 that it had opened an antitrust case: "市场监管总局依法对英伟达公司开展立案调查" [83]. On Jul 31, 2025 the cyberspace regulator summoned Nvidia, "约谈了英伟达公司", over the security risks of vulnerabilities and backdoors in the H20 chips it sells in China [84]. On Sept 15, 2025 the market regulator announced a preliminary finding against Nvidia, and a further investigation: "市场监管总局依法决定对其实施进一步调查" [85]. That notice cites the antimonopoly law and the regulator's 2020 conditions on Nvidia's purchase of Mellanox, and gives no facts. Nvidia's own account of the finding points the other way: "our compliance with applicable U.S. export controls, which required us to offer degraded products to the Chinese market, discriminated unfairly against customers in the China market" [86]. By that account the case faults Nvidia for what it held back from Chinese customers, not for selling to them. The regulator's Party group, in the Party members' paper of Sept 24, 2026, counts the case as political work: "依法对英伟达、高通公司、杜邦中国集团立案调查，在重要时间节点上发布权威声音" (it opened cases against Nvidia, Qualcomm and DuPont China under the law, and spoke with authority at important moments) [87]. Xinhua, in March 2026, had called the same enforcement even-handed: "坚持对各类企业一视同仁" [88]. American bodies read the case as a lever in trade talks. The US-China commission's report of November 2025, citing Reuters and the Financial Times, says the finding came "in the midst of negotiations", as the regulator dropped an inquiry into Google, "signaling China's willingness to both exert leverage and ease pressure as a negotiating tactic" [89]. The White House said on Nov 1, 2025 that "China will terminate its various investigations targeting U.S. companies in the semiconductor supply chain, including its antitrust, anti-monopoly, and anti-dumping investigations" [90]; the commerce ministry's account of the deal names none of China's investigations of American firms and, of such companies, says only "相关企业个案处理" (handling the cases of the companies concerned) [91]. The Trade Representative's report of 2026 says that by the end of 2025 China "had terminated or suspended these investigations", which "were being pursued for retaliatory purposes"; it names no company [92]. No page read says the Nvidia case ended: the regulator's review of its 2025 work, posted Apr 10, 2026, still lists "对英伟达公司实施进一步调查" [93]; its news list to Nov 23, 2026 has a suspension of the case against DuPont China and none for Nvidia [94] [95]; and Nvidia's report of August 2026 still lists the finding among its risks [86].

## 5. Five limits on the claim

**17.** **Beijing was not one actor.** Vice Premier He Lifeng told Nvidia's chief executive in April 2025 that more American firms, Nvidia among them, were welcome to put down deeper roots in China: "欢迎包括英伟达公司在内的更多美资企业深耕中国市场" [155]. The commerce minister told him in July 2025 that he hoped multinationals, Nvidia among them, would give Chinese customers high-quality, reliable products and services: "希望包括英伟达在内的跨国公司为中国客户提供优质可靠的产品和服务" [156]. The Financial Times, Nov 13, 2026, describes the split: the commerce ministry "favoured imports", while security officials in the cyberspace regulator and the security ministry "viewed dependence on Nvidia as a strategic vulnerability"; "Over the past year, the latter position has gained ground." [157] The US Trade Representative said in November 2026 that the Chinese side is not monolithic (point 12). In the same days as the first of the summer's instructions (point 14), People's Daily Overseas Edition (Jul 22, 2025) printed an interview in which a university scholar, Zhang Xin, advised that "国内企业的最优策略是“双线并行”" (the best strategy for Chinese firms is two lines at once): "一边积极申请、储备进口高端芯片" (on one side, actively apply for and stock imported high-end chips), on the other fill the gap with algorithms and home-made chips [158]. That is a scholar's printed advice, not an act of the state. On May 14, 2026, meeting the American executives who came with the President, Xi said: "中方欢迎美国对华加强互利合作，相信美国企业在华将拥有更广阔前景" (China welcomes more mutually beneficial American cooperation and believes American firms will have broader prospects in China) [159]. Premier Li Qiang told them, Nvidia's among them, that a stable and open policy environment is the Chinese government's long-standing commitment: "一个稳定开放的政策环境是中国政府长期不变的承诺" [160]. Reuters, Jan 30, 2026, reporting that DeepSeek too had been approved: "China's industry and commerce ministries have granted approvals for all four companies" (DeepSeek, ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent), with the conditions left to the state planner [161]. The commerce ministry also eased Chinese firms' computing abroad: presenting on Oct 16, 2026 a draft that adds 23 AI technologies, computing clusters among them, to the catalogue of technologies restricted from export, it said exports of technology for "中国企业在境外自建自营算力中心" (Chinese firms' own computing centres abroad) would get "适用自动许可等便利措施" (automatic licences and other facilitation) [162], and on Nov 17, 2026 it called the new American curbs on remote access interference in the "正常贸易合作" (normal trade cooperation) of Chinese firms with firms of other countries [163]. Local voucher rules paid for computing that is not home-made too: of the 15 counted, 11 pay one rate whatever the chip and 4 pay a lower rate than for home-made (point 3). So the conflict ran through the state itself: its security and planning side against the companies, with the commerce side nearer to the companies.

**18.** **Guidance and public warnings, not a published ban.** No published Chinese order against buying Nvidia chips was found in the 103 policy pages read. Such an order could have been found: the 2023 review conclusion telling operators of critical information infrastructure to stop buying Micron's products is among those pages, "应停止采购美光公司产品" [164]. And when the finance ministry published a purchase bar by name, on Jun 22, 2026, "不得采购46家美国企业（不包括在华美资企业）生产的产品" (government buyers may not buy products made by 46 American firms, leaving out American-funded firms in China), its list, of defence and aerospace firms, named no chip maker [165] [166]. The purchase rules in point 2 bind Party and government bodies, and the same sentence adds "其他单位可不在采购需求中提出此项要求" (other units may leave this requirement out) [13]. Every home-made percentage found in a published text is a province's or a city's; the national computing-network opinions of December 2023 set none [167]. A draft national plan for AI data centres with "at least 80 per cent of the core technology, AI chips included" from local suppliers was reported by Bloomberg in June 2026, from unnamed people [168]. Asked on Jun 18, 2026 about foreign reports of large investment in a computing network, the state planner's Li Chao gave no investment figure and no home-made share, and said "在具体建设过程中，市场力量将起决定性作用；我们将会同有关部门，加强统筹协调和政策引导" (in the actual building, market forces will play the decisive role; we will, with the departments concerned, strengthen overall coordination and policy guidance) [169]. Bloomberg's report of September 2024 said it in terms: "The policy has taken the form of guidance rather than an outright ban" [123]. The first reports of the summer's guidance said the same in August 2025 (point 14). The Financial Times's report of Sept 17, 2025 does call it a ban, in its headline, "China bans tech companies from buying Nvidia's AI chips", and in its text, "The ban goes beyond earlier guidance from regulators"; what it describes is an order told to companies, on the word of three unnamed people. It reports no published rule, and none was found [129]. The published texts found stop short of an order: the commentaries and the industry body's call in point 4 warn and urge, and none forbids. The state-media warnings came in one burst: of 42 state and Party media items on Nvidia chips found from July 2025 to November 2026, the seven that only warn about Nvidia's chips are dated Aug 1 to Sept 24, 2025, the last a Science and Technology Daily report of a forum at which an academician urged government and enterprise users to speed up home-made substitution [46] [49] (tables/state_media_items.tsv). In the answers found, Beijing's spokespeople confirmed nothing: "我不了解你提到的情况" (I am not aware of what you mention), said the foreign ministry on Aug 13, 2025 [170] and the commerce ministry on Mar 19 and Jul 16, 2026 [171] [172]; "具体问题建议向中方主管部门了解" (for specifics, I suggest you ask the competent Chinese departments), said the foreign ministry on Sept 18, 2025 [173], and in like words on Jan 29 and Nov 9, 2026 [174] [175]. A Hong Kong paper's account of a Financial Times report, Aug 19, 2026, says purchases "需通過國家發改委審批" (must pass the state planner's approval) [176]: an approval step is reported, though no rule for it was found. A 2020 antitrust decision even obliges Nvidia to go on supplying its GPU accelerators to China on fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory terms, "向中国市场继续供应英伟达GPU加速器", unless the conditions have since been lifted [177]. So Luckey's "China will not give them an import permit" [1] says more than the record shows: no permit rule for these chips was found, and Reuters's words are that Beijing "withholds approval for large-scale imports" [136].

**19.** **Some purchases were approved, and some chips came in.** Reuters, Jan 28, 2026: ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent "have been approved to purchase more than 400,000 H200 chips in total" [139]. The conditions are reported two ways. Lianhe Zaobao, the same day, citing an earlier Bloomberg report: Chinese regulators "要求上述三家科技企业需采购一定比例的国产晶片" (required the three companies to buy a share of home-made chips) as one condition [178]. Bloomberg's own Japanese excerpt of its report of Jan 23 says the authorities will urge companies to buy home-made chips, and sets no amount: "当局は承認の条件として、一定量の国産半導体チップ購入を企業に促す方針だが、具体的な数量は設定されていない" (as a condition of the approval, the authorities intend to urge companies to buy a certain amount of home-made chips, but no amount is set) [179]. Reuters, Mar 17, 2026: "Nvidia has won Beijing's approval to sell its second-most powerful artificial intelligence chips to China" [180]. Nvidia's report for the quarter to Jul 26, 2026 says the licensed sales "were restricted by the PRC government", that it took a $0.4 billion charge "as the demand for H200 products diminished", and then: "we have made a fraction of the allowed shipments under the USG's H200 licensing program" [86]. The Financial Times, through Reuters, Aug 18, 2026: "Small batches of Nvidia's H200 chips, one of the company's most powerful AI chips, have been allowed to enter mainland China"; ByteDance and Tencent "have each received about 10,000 H200 processors in recent weeks", while "Beijing wants them to keep the hardware outside mainland China", and regulators told companies they could ship the chips to Hong Kong and use them there [141]. Tencent's chief strategy officer, Mar 18, 2026, spoke of buying "higher-end imported GPUs which are now becoming available again" alongside more Chinese-designed ones [181]. Nvidia sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper products to China in the quarter ended Oct 25, 2026 (section 4) and said on its call: "we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper Data Center products to China" [142]. It gave no reason; Reuters wrote that its interim chief executive "did not explain why Nvidia had stopped shipping the processors or whether the halt would be permanent" [143]. She told CNBC the same day that the licensed H200s "went to China within this last quarter" [152]. None of these says how many of those chips crossed into the mainland. Other Nvidia chips were sold, or were reported to have arrived, too. Nvidia's annual report, of the H20 licences of August 2025: "We generated approximately $60 million in H20 revenue under those licenses." [101] iSoftStone's own record of its results meeting of Jun 25, 2026 says its Shanghai node built a new H20 inference cluster, "上海节点新建 H20 推理集群" [182]; National Business Daily reported on Oct 13, 2026 that a person close to the company confirmed 8,000 Nvidia cards of the 6000D model had arrived [183]. Two universities posted notices to buy that card in July 2026, each holding suppliers to the government procurement law's conditions: Beihang, asking for quotes for four, "品牌 NVIDIA 规格型号 RTX PRO 6000D" [184], and Wuhan University, by competitive bidding, for the "国行合规RTX PRO 6000D型号" (the compliant China-market model) [185]; a notice to buy is neither a purchase made nor a delivery.

**20.** **The companies had reasons of their own, and American curbs and conditions pushed too.** Their own chips and costs first. Alibaba says its "chip design subsidiary T-Head has brought proprietary GPUs into scaled production" [186]; its chief executive judged in November 2026, "结合当前形势" (in light of the present situation), that from 2027 on "公司绝大部分新增AI芯片供给将以自研平头哥国产芯片为主" (most of the company's added AI chip supply will be its own T-Head chips), with other home-made chips bought for part of its inference [187]. Baidu runs a cluster "comprising 30,000 of its self-developed, third generation P800 Kunlun chips" [188]. The founder of 360, Jul 23, 2025: its recent purchases "都是华为的产品" (are all Huawei's), and for inference "国产芯片性价比更高" (home-made chips are better value) than the H20; he granted that they trail Nvidia's but said they must be used, "只有咬牙坚持用" (only by gritting one's teeth and sticking with them) [189]. Zhipu, on the US Entity List since Jan 16, 2025 [190], says that in coding work the per-token inference cost on home-made chips "has reached a level comparable in cost-performance to mainstream imported GPUs", and gives its own reason: "Given the persistent global supply constraints on compute, this path ensures that the Company's cost curve remains within its own control" [191]. China Daily, counted among the state media of point 4, in a report of Aug 15, 2025, in the weeks of those warnings, quoted an engineer at an internet company it did not name: "Cybersecurity is of crucial importance to us. We cannot tolerate any potential risks. Even if H20 chips are available now, we are not that eager to buy." [192] Reuters, Sept 16, 2025: the RTX Pro 6000D (Reuters writes RTX6000D) met "only lukewarm demand", being "seen as expensive for what it does", in a report that also says "Beijing has become increasingly keen for Chinese firms to switch to domestic chips even if Nvidia's products are preferred by many companies" [128]; the Financial Times, the next day: "Several companies had indicated they would order tens of thousands of the RTX Pro 6000D" [129]. American curbs pushed too. Reuters reported in November 2023 that Baidu had ordered Huawei chips that August, ahead of new US curbs, "to prepare for a future where they may no longer be able to purchase from Nvidia" [193]. Tencent's president, Martin Lau, said the same month of the new curbs: "And we will also try to look for domestic source for these training chips." [194] In November 2026 he said the new US curbs on computing abroad left everyone in China with less advanced compute (point 9). The US-listed Chinese companies whose annual reports speak of chip supply put the risk on export controls (point 11). A Chinese chip maker's prospectus says "地缘政治倒逼相关国内客户使用国产 GPU 产品" (geopolitics forces these domestic customers to use home-made GPUs), right after a list of American export rules [195]. Hanna Dohmen, a research analyst at Georgetown University's CSET, in her written statement for a US-China commission hearing of Feb 6, 2025: "export controls are creating increased demand for indigenous Chinese equipment, fabrication capacity, and AI chips" [69]. The commission's own report of November 2026, of the state: "U.S. and allied controls did not drive China's indigenization efforts, but they have added urgency in recent years." [196] Nvidia, in every filing since November 2025: "we are already effectively foreclosed from the China market by U.S. export controls" [4]. The H200 also met obstacles on the American side. The Commerce Department's rule of Jan 15, 2026 requires that "the item undergoes independent, third-party testing in the United States" and that "the aggregate shipments of the product to China and Macau will be no more than 50% of the total product shipped to customers for end use in the United States of that product" [197]. Nvidia: chips shipped under the licences "are subject to a 25% tariff upon importation into the United States", and "We have been unable to pass along any of the tariff to our customers" [86]. Its spokesperson in February 2026, of the US licence conditions: "the conditions need to be commercially practical, else the market will continue to move to foreign alternatives" [198]. Reuters, relaying the Financial Times, Feb 3, 2026: Chinese customers were "not placing H200 chip orders with Nvidia until it becomes clear whether they will be able to secure the licences" [199]. And Washington returned without action 55% of the chips in the applications its summary of May 11, 2026 records, among them every application for a consignee in Malaysia, Indonesia or Thailand, each named for ByteDance, BytePlus, TikTok or Alibaba (section 4).

**21.** **What Beijing meant by it is not settled.** No Chinese document found states the motive. Several American bodies read parts of Beijing's conduct, the antitrust case above all, as leverage in the trade talks: the US-China commission in November 2025, the White House on Nov 1, 2025, and the Trade Representative's report of 2026, which calls China's antimonopoly investigations retaliation (point 10) [89] [90] [92]. Among China's commitments in the Busan deal the White House listed: "End Chinese retaliation against U.S. semiconductor manufacturers and other major U.S. companies." [90] The Congressional Research Service, September 2025, read the pressure as a push for more chips, not fewer: China "may use its purported security concerns about the H20 to press for technology disclosures and access to more advanced chips, such as Nvidia's B30 chip", and the market regulator's finding of that month was "another effort to pressure Nvidia to provide its advanced chips to China" [200]. The Trade Representative reads it the other way, in three interviews on Bloomberg Television (captions, not checked by ear). Of Washington's H200 decision: "That was not a negotiated outcome." (Dec 19, 2025, 5:35) [201] Whether Chinese companies may buy is "a sovereign decision for China", and "They're very committed to domestic production." (May 15, 2026, 5:59 to 6:27) [8] Asked whether Xi raised chips on the President's trip: "But the Chinese side did not raise this issue with us." (Nov 20, 2026, 0:28 to 0:34) [112] The American side had put chips on the table itself: asked why the April block on the H20 was lifted, the Treasury Secretary said "You might say that that was a negotiating chip that we used in Geneva and in London" (a clip Bloomberg Television aired on Jul 15, 2025; captions, 8:55) [202], and the President wrote on Dec 8, 2025 that he had told Xi the H200 would be allowed: "President Xi responded positively!" [203] That is the President's account, not Xi's words. Whether the Nvidia case has ended is not known (point 10). As to the dates: the cyberspace regulator summoned Nvidia on Jul 31, 2025, two days after the Stockholm talks of Jul 28 and 29 [84] [204]; the antitrust finding came on Sept 15, the second day of the Madrid talks [85] [205]. By my count over the figure's own list (tables/talks_and_acts.tsv), 7 of the 13 restrictive acts fall within 7 days of a meeting in person, where about a quarter of all days do; the easing acts do not cluster (2 of 9, one of the two being the scholar's interview of point 17). But restrictions also followed deals: the order on state-funded data centres was reported on Nov 5, 2025, six days after the Busan meeting [131] [206], and the planner's guidance on home-made chips and the AI-chip evaluation list came a week and more after the Beijing summit of May 14, 2026 [45] [15] [207]. Some dates are those of press reports, the acts come in bursts, and a count of dates cannot show a motive. None of the 30 official readouts of the talks saved names Nvidia, the H20 or the H200, though the same search finds export controls in some. Under either reading the direction stands: what is open is what Beijing wanted from its acts, not which way they pulled the companies.

## 6. What can be said, and how

- **Flat, on documents and filings.** China's declared policy is to end dependence on foreign core technology and to raise the share of home-made chips (points 1 to 3). Its regulators opened a case against Nvidia and summoned it (point 10). Nvidia told securities regulators in November 2025 that the Chinese government "restricted customers from purchasing, importing, or using our data center products" (the annual report says "discouraged" [101]), and, two sentences later, that "we are already effectively foreclosed from the China market by U.S. export controls" [4]. Alibaba told them the cyberspace regulator asked companies to halt purchases of certain chips (point 11). A Shanghai government body says internet companies' clusters in the city run mainly on Nvidia (point 5). By May 11, 2026 Washington had approved 879,868 of Nvidia's and AMD's chips, none yet shipped, and returned 1,888,950 without action (section 4). Nvidia's revenue from customers based in China fell by about two thirds during 2025 and was, in the quarter ended Oct 25, 2026, the highest of the nine quarters reported on that basis; that is not a count of chips entering China (section 4).
- **In public, as warnings and urging, not as orders.** The security ministry's warning, the state-media commentaries (found from August to September 2025), the state broadcaster's account saying buyers could choose not to buy the H20, and an industry body's call for caution (points 4 and 18). Nothing read ties the state-media texts to the guidance the press reports, and a Party paper also printed a scholar's advice to stock imported chips (point 17).
- **As reported, from unnamed people.** Who gave the instructions to stop buying, and when; the warning about traitors; the customs checks; the 600,000 chips in Hong Kong; every number in the first table of section 4 except the United States government's count and Nvidia's own.
- **As others read it.** That Beijing's acts were leverage in trade talks, or a sovereign choice with no bargain in them (point 21).
- **Not to be said.** That China banned Nvidia chips: no published ban was found, and some chips came in. That an import permit was refused: no permit rule was found. That Beijing spoke with one voice: its commerce side and its security and planning side pulled apart (point 17). That the companies wanted only Nvidia: they also build and buy home-made chips. That the antitrust case shows Beijing wanted no Nvidia sales: by Nvidia's account the finding is that it offered Chinese customers degraded products. That the case was a bargaining move, or that it was not: both are readings (point 21). That only Beijing stood in the H200's way: Washington's conditions and a 25% tariff did too, and Washington returned without action 55% of the chips in the applications its summary of May 11, 2026 records. That Washington refused the chips it returned: the table gives no reason.

**One sentence that survives all five limits.** From 2024 the security and planning side of the Chinese state pressed China's AI companies, through unpublished guidance that the press reported, to buy home-made chips rather than Nvidia's, and from the summer of 2025 to stop ordering them, while the security ministry warned in public of backdoors in chips made abroad and state media warned about Nvidia's chips, most of all in August and September 2025; other parts of the state welcomed Nvidia and approved some purchases; the companies went on ordering Nvidia chips in the millions and, by the reports, had received a small part of them by late 2026; they were also turning to home-made chips, for reasons of their own and under American curbs; and whether Beijing meant its acts as lasting policy or as leverage in trade talks is read both ways.
