China chip policy · Palmer Luckey's clip against the record

Right, and too flat

Palmer Luckey's clip on China and Nvidia chips, its words from 0:00 to 0:51, set against the record. He has the direction right. He is too flat on who China is, on what, by the reports, held chips back, on what is known about purchase approvals and licensed sales and on who else stood in the way; and what Beijing meant by it is not settled.

“China doesn't want NVIDIA to sell GPUs to Chinese companies.” (0:11) 1Borne out in direction, for 2025 and 2026. Alibaba's annual report to the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC): “In September 2025, the Cyberspace Administration of China requested Chinese technology companies to halt purchases of certain chips.” 2 That sentence does not name Nvidia. Nvidia told the SEC that its licensed H200 sales “were restricted by the PRC government” 3.Too broad as a constant. China was not one actor. Its commerce minister told Nvidia's chief executive in July 2025 that he hoped multinationals, Nvidia among them, would give Chinese customers high-quality, reliable products and services 4. The Financial Times reported in November 2026, from unnamed people, that the commerce ministry “favoured imports” while security officials “viewed dependence on Nvidia as a strategic vulnerability” 5.

“China will not give them an import permit” (0:25) 1A press report, not a rule. The same report, from unnamed people, describes the reported stockpile in its own words “as Beijing withholds approval for large-scale imports despite demand from technology firms” 10. On the record, Nvidia said on its results call of May 20, 2026: “we are uncertain whether any imports will be allowed into the country” 11.It says more than the record shows. No permit rule for these chips was found in China's lists of goods that need an import licence for 2024, 2025 and 2026, read whole (the 2026 list: 12), and no published ban was found in the 82 Chinese policy pages read. Bloomberg's report of September 2024 called the pressure “guidance rather than an outright ban” 13. Reuters reported conditional approvals for “more than 400,000 H200 chips in total” in January 2026, citing four people it did not name 14. In August 2026, the Financial Times, citing two people it did not name, reported that small batches “have been allowed to enter mainland China”, as Reuters relayed it 15. Nvidia separately reported licensed sales to China in its filing for the quarter to Jul 26, 2026, saying “we have made a fraction of the allowed shipments under the USG's H200 licensing program” 3. Nor was Beijing the only obstacle: Reuters wrote on May 14, 2026 that the “path to a completed sale has been obstructed by a tangle of requirements on both sides” 16.
“because they want to build an internal domestic processor industry, silicon industry, not build one that is dependent on the United States” (0:25 to 0:35) 1Borne out as the declared policy. In May 2026 Li Chao, of the state planner's policy research office, said it was guiding home-made large models to do more to adapt to home-made computing chips 17. The US President, Donald Trump, gave his own account of it on May 15, 2026: asked “But they haven't bought any so far?”, he said: “No, because they chose not to. They want to try and develop their own.” 18The motive is not settled. Beijing's own texts give purposes for its public acts: security for the cyberspace regulator's summons of Nvidia, and national interests and fair competition for the market regulator's cases, Nvidia's among them 192021. No Chinese document found states a motive for the unpublished guidance to companies, or ties a Beijing act against Nvidia to the trade talks. Of the customs measures it reported in January 2026, citing people it did not name, Reuters wrote that it “remains unclear whether Beijing wants to ban it outright so that domestic chip companies can flourish”, or “whether these measures could be used as a bargaining tactic in talks with Washington” 22. The US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, in its report to Congress, saw in the move against Nvidia of China's market regulator, the State Administration for Market Regulation, “China's willingness to both exert leverage and ease pressure as a negotiating tactic” 23. The US Trade Representative, Jamieson Greer, points the other way: asked whether China would let its companies buy the H200, he said it was “gonna be a sovereign decision for China”, and “They're very committed to domestic production.” (captions, not checked by ear, 5:59 to 6:27) 24. Nvidia's chief executive said in May 2026 that Beijing had to weigh protecting its home market against expanding its AI capacity 25. The direction of the policy stands under each of the three readings of what Beijing meant by it (point 21).
“Jensen was basically on the side of Chinese AI companies, but not the Chinese government.” (0:47 to 0:51; Jensen Huang is Nvidia's chief executive) 1Fair on the import question in 2025 and 2026, by the reports. The companies wanted the chips. Reuters, citing people it did not name, reported that Beijing “withholds approval for large-scale imports” 10. American requirements stood in the way too (see the row on the import permit). Whose side Huang himself was on is Luckey's reading, and this sheet does not weigh it.It leaves out the purchase approvals Reuters reported. Reuters, Jan 30, 2026 (on Yahoo Finance), from unnamed people, of DeepSeek, ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent: “China's industry and commerce ministries have granted approvals for all four companies” 26.

The same point, said so that it holds

Reuters reported the orders and purchase approvals 814, and the Financial Times reported the receipts 27. Both outlets cited people they did not name. Nvidia's own filings report licensed sales to China 3.

From 2024 the planning side of the Chinese state and the industry ministry, and from 2025 its security side, pressed China's AI companies, through unpublished guidance that the press reported, to buy home-made chips rather than Nvidia's, and from the summer of 2025 to stop ordering them, while the security ministry warned in public of backdoors in chips made abroad and state media warned about Nvidia's chips, most of all from the end of July to September 2025; published texts of provinces, cities and a region, and, by a city's reply, a science-ministry guide, set shares, quotas or conditions that favour home-made computing, none of them a ban; other parts of the state welcomed Nvidia and, by the reports, approved some purchases; the companies went on ordering Nvidia chips, in the millions by Reuters's reports, and the Financial Times reported in August 2026 that some H200s had been received, each outlet citing people it did not name; they were also turning to home-made chips, for reasons of their own and under American curbs; and whether Beijing meant its acts as lasting policy, as leverage in trade talks or as protection of its home market is read all three ways.
