## The answer these would test

From 2024 to 2026 the Chinese state's declared policy, and what its regulators did, pressed China's AI companies to move away from Nvidia chips. The companies went on using those chips, ordering them and asking for them. Five limits apply. First, Beijing was not one actor. Second, the pressure came as public warnings and unpublished guidance, not as a published ban. Third, some purchases were approved and some chips came in. Fourth, the companies had reasons of their own, and American curbs and conditions pushed too. Fifth, what Beijing meant by it is not settled.

Where a document bears on one of the limits, its entry says which. The direction itself rests on documents already held (see the last section), and it stands under either reading of what Beijing meant by it.

## Documents from Beijing

### 1. A published Chinese rule or notice on importing these chips

- **What it would show.** A rule would show whether bringing an H200 into the mainland needs a permit; a decision on one company's application would show whether a permit was refused. It bears on the second limit. It would also test Luckey's words "China will not give them an import permit" [1] (a machine transcript of the clip, at 0:25; not checked by ear), which say more than the record shows.
- **Where it would be.** On the site of the commerce ministry or of the customs administration, as a list of restricted imports or a licence notice.
- **Held now instead.** No such rule was found in the 82 policy pages read, nor in China's own import lists: the lists of goods that need an import licence, an automatic import licence or a dual-use import licence, for 2024, 2025 and 2026, read whole by script, name no computer, computer part or chip (no customs number under headings 8471, 8473 or 8542), though the same search finds the computers and chips that the dual-use list controls for export [2] [3] [4]; nor do the lists of banned imports in force [5]; the sixth batch, of 2005, lists 17 kinds of asbestos and chemicals [6]. The announcements of the customs administration and of the Customs Tariff Commission found for January 2024 to November 2026, read whole with their annexes by a search of Nov 25, 2026, set no licence, hold or chip-specific inspection on importing these goods, and none names Nvidia or a chip model; that search is not a census of every numbered announcement. The Foreign Trade Law does allow a restriction outside the lists: the commerce ministry, with the State Council's approval and on the grounds the law lists, may "临时决定限制或者禁止前款规定目录以外的特定货物、技术的进口或者出口" (decide for a time to restrict or ban the import or export of particular goods and technologies outside the catalogues) [7], in the words of the law as amended in 2022; the law as revised on Dec 27, 2025, in force from Mar 1, 2026, keeps the power, "临时决定禁止或者限制前款规定目录以外的特定货物、技术的进出口" (decide for a time to ban or restrict the import and export of particular goods and technologies outside the catalogues), and among its grounds are national security and "为建立或者加快建立国内特定产业的" (to build, or speed the building of, a particular domestic industry) [8]; no such decision naming these chips was found. The Customs Law says that restricted goods without licence documents are not released, "国家限制进出口的货物,没有进出口许可证件的,不予放行" [9], but none of the pages read is a list that names these chips. On Jul 30, 2026 a company asked the commerce ministry whether such an import needs a special licence, "进口是否需要办理专用进口许可证" [10]. The ministry's reply of Aug 19, 2026 speaks only of exporters and export controls; it does not answer the question about imports. One listed company, 智微智能 (stock code 001339), which buys AI servers made in part by foreign makers, told the Shenzhen exchange in September 2026 that it had compared them with three Chinese catalogues, of technologies banned or restricted from import, of goods under import licence, and of dual-use items and technologies under import and export licence: "智算服务器及配套设备不涉及目录项下禁止或限制进口的产品或技术" (the servers and their equipment are not products or technologies banned or restricted from import under the catalogues); the reply names no maker of those servers or of their chips [11]. A Hong Kong paper's account of a Financial Times report, Aug 19, 2026, says purchases "需通過國家發改委審批" (must pass the state planner's approval) [12]: an approval step is reported, though no rule for it was found. Of another Nvidia card, the RTX PRO 5500, Reuters reported on Nov 23, 2026, from two unnamed people, that large purchases by internet companies "could require Chinese government approval" [13]. In its report of Nov 5, 2026 that H200 chips were stored in Hong Kong, Reuters's words are that Beijing "withholds approval for large-scale imports" [14]. On the record, Nvidia said on its results call of May 20, 2026: "we are uncertain whether any imports will be allowed into the country" [15]. That sentence names no rule and no authority.

### 2. The instructions to the companies, in writing

- **What it would show.** Which body told which companies what, about which chips, and whether any paper exists at all. It bears on the second limit: the instructions are known from press reports citing unnamed people and from one sentence in a company filing that does not name Nvidia.
- **Where it would be.** With the cyberspace regulator, the industry ministry or the state planner (NDRC), the bodies the reports name. A text of this kind has been published before. In 2023 the Cybersecurity Review Office published, on the cyberspace regulator's site, its conclusion on Micron's products: operators of critical information infrastructure "应停止采购美光公司产品" (shall stop buying Micron's products) [16].
- **Looked for.** By the argument's own searches (its point 11): of the latest annual reports of fifteen US-listed Chinese companies, only Alibaba's says a Chinese authority asked Chinese technology companies to stop buying certain chips; in the 76 results-call transcripts of eight Chinese companies, November 2023 to November 2026, and 42 records of four smaller model makers, no speaker and no record names a Chinese authority as restricting chip purchases, though 27 of the transcripts leave out passages spoken in another language; in Chinese records of 25 of those 27 calls no speaker names a Chinese authority beside a chip word either. For the paper on window guidance below, neither web searches for its words nor the titles on the state planner's and the finance ministry's lists of documents turned up a copy (its point 15).
- **Held now instead.** One sentence in Alibaba's annual report, which does not name Nvidia: "In September 2025, the Cyberspace Administration of China requested Chinese technology companies to halt purchases of certain chips." [17] The Financial Times, Aug 20, 2025, from unnamed people: the cyberspace regulator issued what the paper calls "window guidance", "an informal notice", "instructing them to halt new orders for Nvidia's H20 chips" [18]. The same paper, Sept 17, 2025, by three people it did not name: the regulator "told companies, including ByteDance and Alibaba, this week to end their testing and orders of the RTX Pro 6000D" [19]. A Chinese trade site, Leiphone, in an article the argument dates to Oct 31, 2025, described a newly circulating paper on such guidance: "有最新流传的“窗口指导”相关文件写道" [20]. It gives no issuer, number or date, and no copy of the paper was found.

### 4. A Chinese text that says what Beijing meant by it

- **What it would show.** Whether Beijing meant its acts against Nvidia as lasting policy or as leverage in trade talks. It bears on the fifth limit. It would not change the direction, which stands under either reading: what is open is what Beijing wanted from its acts, not which way they pulled the companies.
- **Where it would be.** A Chinese readout of the leaders' exchanges or of the trade talks would be on the foreign ministry's or the commerce ministry's site, or with the state news agency. The American written readouts would be on the White House's or the Trade Representative's site.
- **Looked for.** By the argument's own account, none of the saved official pages on the talks (the two sides' readouts, joint statements and fact sheets, and notices of a round to come) names Nvidia, the H20 or the H200, nor do the President's own posts on four calls with Xi and on two rounds. One Chinese official text sets the chip beside the talks: asked on Jul 18, 2025 about American officials' remark that approving H20 sales was part of the trade negotiations, the commerce ministry's spokesperson first described what followed the London talks and then said "美方近日又主动表示将批准对华销售英伟达H20芯片" (the US side has recently also said, of its own accord, that it will approve sales of Nvidia's H20 chips to China); the answer neither confirms nor denies that the chip was part of the talks [27]. No American written readout of the rounds in London, Madrid, Kuala Lumpur, Paris and Korea, of the round of September 2026, of the two leaders' own meeting in Shenzhen or of the video calls of Dec 5, 2025 and Apr 30, 2026 was found in the Treasury's indexes of readouts and press releases, the Trade Representative's monthly lists of press releases or the White House's lists of its posts, as read on Nov 24, 2026. The foreign ministry's list of news on the United States names five calls between Xi and the President from January 2025 to November 2026; both sides' accounts of all five are saved, and none names chips.
- **Held now instead.** No Chinese document found states a motive for the unpublished guidance to companies, or ties an act against Nvidia to the trade talks; Beijing's own texts give purposes for its public acts (security, national interests, fair competition). American officials and bodies read it in more than one way. The US-China Economic and Security Review Commission, in its 2025 report to Congress, saw in the move against Nvidia of China's market regulator, the State Administration for Market Regulation, "China's willingness to both exert leverage and ease pressure as a negotiating tactic" [28]. The US Trade Representative, Jamieson Greer, asked whether China would let its companies buy the H200, said it was "gonna be a sovereign decision for China" (captions, not checked by ear, at 5:59) [29]. The President wrote on Dec 8, 2025 that he had told Xi the H200 would be allowed, and: "President Xi responded positively!" [30] That is the President's account, not Xi's words; no Chinese readout of that exchange is among the saved pages.

### 10. Washington's reasons for the applications it returned without action

- **What it would show.** Why Washington returned so many licence applications without action. It bears on the fourth limit: how much of what stood in the H200's way was on the American side.
- **Where it would be.** With the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, or in the fuller papers of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, which made the table public on Nov 19, 2026 [62].
- **Held now instead.** The table of licence applications from the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, with their status on May 11, 2026 [63]. Of the 3,444,160 chips in it, Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X together, 1,888,950 are in applications marked RWA, the bureau's short form for "returned without action (RWA)" [64]: Washington returned without action the applications for 55% of the chips its summary of May 11, 2026 records (the sums are ours, by script, from the table's 54 rows). Another 879,868 are in applications marked "Approved, not shipped" [63]. The table gives no reason; one possible reason is that "The applicant has requested the application be returned" [65]. The committee says some applications are "not fully reflected in the May 11, 2026, summary document": "For example, NVIDIA applied on December 19, 2025, to export 445,000 H200s to Tencent" [66]. The table's only Tencent row of that date is an Nvidia application for 75,000 chips, approved, and neither document says whether it is that application. So the 55% is a share of the table, not of everything applied for: counted with the 445,000, it would be 49.5% if that row is the application, counted at 445,000, and 48.6% if the 445,000 is a further application and was not returned without action (our arithmetic). The bureau's rule published on Nov 19, 2026 adds a licence requirement for advanced chips bound for Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand or Brazil, "countries which present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion", except for companies headquartered, or whose ultimate parent company is headquartered, in the United States, "provided certain reporting requirements are met"; it does not speak of the returned applications [67]. An application names a data-centre operator or ultimate consignee; it is not an order, and an approval there is Washington's licence, not Beijing's.

## What is not open

These parts of the answer rest on documents already held. A new find could add to them; it would not unsay them.

- China's declared policy is to end dependence on foreign core technology and to raise the share of home-made chips.
- Its regulators opened a case against Nvidia and summoned it.
- Nvidia told securities regulators in November 2025 that the Chinese government "restricted customers from purchasing, importing, or using our data center products", and, two sentences later, that "we are already effectively foreclosed from the China market by U.S. export controls" [52]. Alibaba told them the cyberspace regulator asked companies to halt purchases of certain chips.
- A Shanghai government body says the computing clusters that internet companies build themselves in the city run mainly on Nvidia cards.
- By May 11, 2026 Washington had approved applications for 879,868 of Nvidia's and AMD's chips, none yet shipped, and returned without action the applications for 1,888,950 more.
