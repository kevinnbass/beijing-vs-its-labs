## The claim

Reuters, Nov 5, 2026: about 600,000 of Nvidia's H200 chips "are stored in Hong Kong awaiting shipment to mainland China, two people familiar with the matter said, as Beijing withholds approval for large-scale imports" [cn-clips-reuters-hongkong-yahoo]. Reuters calls this "The size of the stockpile of H200 chips, which has not previously been reported" [cn-clips-reuters-hongkong-yahoo]. The report does not say whose chips they are, where in Hong Kong they are, or how they got there. The Nov 5 dateline stands on the copy Yahoo carries; CNA's page of the same report shows only its own date, Nov 6, 2026.

Palmer Luckey gave the same number in a clip posted on Nov 19, 2026: "There's 600,000 H200 GPUs sitting in a port right now", because "China will not give them an import permit" [clip-luckey]. Those words are from a machine transcript of the clip (0:18 to 0:30); nobody has checked them by ear. Both phrases say more than the report his number matches. Reuters's words are "stored in Hong Kong", not in a port, and that Beijing "withholds approval for large-scale imports" [cn-clips-reuters-hongkong-yahoo]. The project's argument records that no permit rule for these chips was found (its point 18).

## The answer in short

- No official figure or statement was found that confirms the count. Reuters asked Hong Kong's government and got no reply. China's foreign ministry, asked about the report, referred the question elsewhere.
- No second news outlet with sources of its own for the count was found. The pages that carry it relay Reuters.
- Hong Kong's own trade figures by tariff heading were available to July 2026. At the press price of about 27,000 dollars a chip the pile would be worth about 16 billion dollars. Since the H200 was allowed, the figures show no inflow of that size from the United States or under the partner line usually read as Taiwan, and no pile building up under the headings an American proclamation names for such chips. One gap: goods passing through on one set of transport papers are not declared, so they are in no Hong Kong figure.
- For August and September 2026 Hong Kong's figures could be reached only as broad divisions. Imports of office machines and computers jumped in August 2026 and exports jumped in September. Over the two months together, exports ran ahead of imports by about as much as in two average months of the year. That shows no lasting build-up to the end of September, in a division that holds every maker's machines. It cannot say what came and went, and October was not yet there.
- Taiwan's own figures run to October 2026. They show 4.69 billion US dollars of computers and computer parts sent to Hong Kong in ten months, every maker's goods together: no flow of the size claimed. Its figure for integrated circuits is far too large to tell anything.
- What fits the report is on the record but indirect: Nvidia says much of its product is warehoused in Hong Kong, and it booked a charge for excess H200 stock in the half year to Jul 26, 2026. Nvidia's own figure for licensed sales to China in the quarter to Oct 25, 2026 is 2.4 billion dollars; at the press price the pile would be worth nearly seven times that.
- No customs line, in Hong Kong or anywhere else, can count Nvidia chips: each holds every maker's goods, and no saved page says under which line these chips are declared.

## 1. How large the claim is

1. **About 16 billion US dollars.** Reuters gave a price in January 2026: H200 chips "priced at around $27,000 each" [cn-acts-reuters-customs-20260114]. That is a press figure from unnamed people. At that price 600,000 chips are 16.2 billion US dollars (our multiplication), or about 126 billion Hong Kong dollars at 7.8 to the US dollar (the rate is from my general knowledge of Hong Kong's peg, not from a saved page). Every dollar comparison in this note rests on that price.

2. **Far more than Nvidia says it sold to China under licence.** Nvidia's own figures are on the record. For the quarter to Jul 26, 2026: "Shipments of Data Center Hopper products to China during the second quarter of fiscal year 2027 were less than 1% of Data Center revenue", and "Data Center revenue was $89.0 billion" [man-ms-sec-10q-2026-07]. For the quarter to Oct 25, 2026: "Q3 sales of licensed Hopper Data Center products to China was $2.4 billion" [cn-o38-nvda-q3fy27-call-transcript-pdf]. Before those two quarters it had no revenue under the H200 licences: on May 20, 2026 Nvidia said of them "we have yet to generate any revenue" [cn-o38-nvda-q1fy27-call-transcript-pdf]. The two quarters together are under 3.3 billion dollars (our arithmetic). At the press price of 27,000 dollars a chip, 2.4 billion is about 89,000 chips, and 3.3 billion is about a fifth of what 600,000 chips would be worth (our divisions; Hopper products may be more than H200s alone). So if the price is right, most of such a pile had not been sold to China under licence by Oct 25, 2026. The sources do not say whether the chips in Hong Kong had been sold at all, or whether Nvidia counts a delivery to Hong Kong as a shipment to China.

## 2. Would Hong Kong's trade figures show such a pile?

3. **In principle, yes, with one gap.** Hong Kong's statistics office says of its figures: "Import/export declaration is also required of Hong Kong's trade with Chinese Mainland, and statistics relating to this are included in Hong Kong's external merchandise trade statistics." [cn-le18-hk-csd-410-50031] So goods that leave Hong Kong for the mainland are counted as exports. Hong Kong's answers to a United Nations questionnaire on how it compiles trade figures say: "Hong Kong is a free port." [cn-o42-un-hk-trade-practice] They also say there are no bonded warehouses, that the general trade system is used, and that goods merely passing through are left out. The page is old: its date of release is Apr 7, 2003. If that still holds, chips brought into Hong Kong and stored there were counted as imports when they arrived, and they will be counted as exports when they leave. The gap is goods in passage. Hong Kong's statistics office lists "transhipment cargo consigned on a through bill of lading" or a through air waybill among the goods that need no declaration [cn-o42-hk-csd-declarations-guide]. Its slides for a United Nations workshop in December 2006 define the term as "any article that is brought into Hong Kong under a through bill of lading or a through air waybill from a place outside Hong Kong to another place outside Hong Kong", and say such goods are "exempted from declaration" [cn-o42-hk-csd-un-bangkok-slides]. Both documents are old. Chips held in Hong Kong on through papers to the mainland would be in no Hong Kong trade figure. No source found here says on what papers these chips are held.

The White House's fact sheet names the H200 among those chips: "such as the NVIDIA H200 and AMD MI325X" [cn-o34-us232-factsheet-wh]. The companion note on customs figures (idea 34) sets this out. Imports are also recorded by where the goods were made: Hong Kong answers "Country of origin." [cn-o42-un-hk-trade-practice] Reuters says of the H200: "The chip is manufactured by Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC)" [cn-labs-h200-lobby]. 6. **No inflow of that size since the H200 was allowed.** Reuters says "The United States barred exports of the H200 to China before" the permission of late 2025 [cn-clips-evidence-1]. The Commerce Department dates the President's announcement to "December 8, 2025" [cn-o34-bis-press-rule]. Nvidia dates the first licences with the words "Beginning in February 2026", and its report writes of these controls as covering "China (including Hong Kong and Macau)" [man-ms-sec-10q-2026-07]. If Hong Kong was treated as China for the H200 too, as that wording suggests, the chips could not lawfully be sent there before the licences of 2026. From February to July 2026 Hong Kong's imports under these headings of United States origin and under Other Asia, nes together were 3.1 billion dollars (our sum), against the 16.2 billion dollars that 600,000 chips would be at the press price. The highest month of United States origin was March 2026, at 226.1 million dollars [cn-o34-comtrade-m-202603]. The large 2025 figure under Other Asia, nes in the table includes 4.1 billion dollars in March 2025 alone; that was before the H200 was allowed, and the figures do not say what the goods were. The United States' own figures agree: its exports to Hong Kong under the three headings came to 1,088.4 million dollars over the eight months January to August 2026 (our sum of eight monthly answers, the last of them [cn-o34-comtrade-m-202608]).

7. **Imports under these headings did rise, mostly from mainland China and South Korea.** The average month was 4.9 billion dollars in 2025 and 7.0 billion in January to July 2026. By origin, the average month went from 2.4 to 3.9 billion for mainland China, from 0.35 to 1.1 billion for South Korea and from 0.10 to 0.16 billion for the United States, and fell from 0.95 to 0.35 billion under Other Asia, nes (our averages, from the same monthly answers) [cn-o34-comtrade-m-202607].

8. **No pile visible.** A pile building up would show as imports running ahead of exports. Under these headings it is the other way round in each of the 19 months: exports were larger, by 0.5 to 3.6 billion dollars a month [cn-o34-comtrade-m-202607]. The comparison is rough. The figures do not explain the gap, and it moves by a billion dollars or more from month to month. It does not rule out a pile. Most of what left went to the mainland: of Hong Kong's total exports under the three headings, 65.9 billion dollars in January to July 2026, 48.1 billion went to the mainland (our sums). By heading, its exports to the mainland under 8473.30 were 16.46 billion dollars in January to July 2025 and 36.90 billion in January to July 2026; under 8471.80 they were 12.75 and 7.02 billion, and under 8471.50, 3.90 and 4.19 billion. These lines can show that computer parts and units of every maker went on from Hong Kong to the mainland in tens of billions of dollars. They cannot show whether any Nvidia chip was among them. Hong Kong's statistics office also publishes a quarterly table of selected products that gives parts and accessories of computer hardware on their own; it does not list the tariff codes it covers. In every quarter from January 2024 to June 2026 the total exports of these parts were larger than the imports, by 18,781 million Hong Kong dollars in the first quarter of 2026 and by 39,961 million in the second (our subtractions) [cn-le18-hk-csd-410-96001]. 11. **What that can and cannot tell.** At the press price, and at 7.8 Hong Kong dollars to the US dollar, 600,000 chips would be about 126 billion Hong Kong dollars: more than this division's imports in any month from January 2025 to September 2026. A pile building up inside the division would show as imports gaining on exports. In August 2026 they did: exports exceeded imports by 5,111 million Hong Kong dollars, against 28,302 million in July. In September the excess was 32,089 million. The two months together come to 37,200 million, about two average months of January to September 2026, at 19,270 million each (our arithmetic, from the monthly tables). So the narrowing of August was made up in September, and the division shows no lasting build-up to the end of September 2026. Over the whole of 2025 the average excess was 8,340 million a month: in 2026 exports ran further ahead of imports, not less. The division cannot say what the goods were. It holds every office machine and computer of every maker, the exports of a month are not the imports of that month, and goods held in passage are outside it (point 3). October 2026 was not in the tables. The office also warns: "The sums of the monthly figures may differ from the cumulative totals" [cn-le18-hk-csd-410-50031]. Hong Kong's statistics office offers a tool to build tables by single heading (read on its statistics page, not saved; that it reaches single headings is from my general knowledge). Our reader could not open the tool (HTTP 499) and we did not script it.

Taiwan's group for integrated circuits is too large to tell anything: 60.4 billion dollars to Hong Kong in the ten months. The United States' own exports to Hong Kong are in point 6. None of these shows an inflow of the size claimed. None can rule one out: licensed chips have to leave from the United States, whose figures here end in August 2026, and Hong Kong's own figures leave out goods in passage.

**On the record**

A large Nvidia stock in Hong Kong would be ordinary in itself.

17. **Nvidia booked a charge for excess H200 stock.** The same report, for the quarter to Jul 26, 2026: "we incurred a $0.4 billion charge associated with H200 for excess inventory and purchase obligations" [man-ms-sec-10q-2026-07]. - No news outlet with its own sources for the 600,000. The pages surfaced in four searches relay Reuters, for example The Next Web: "The news agency cited two people familiar with the matter." [ms-bj-202611-h200-hongkong-tnw] Others were read in search results only and not saved.

## What a reader may conclude

- The 600,000 is one report on the word of two unnamed people. Nothing official found here confirms it.
- From February to July 2026, Hong Kong's figures by heading show no inflow of that size under the headings named for these chips, with a chip valued at the press price of about 27,000 dollars. Its monthly divisions show no lasting build-up of computers to the end of September 2026, and Taiwan's figures show no flow of that size to Hong Kong to October 2026. If the chips went through US inspection, as the licences require, and were declared under those headings, nearly all of them left the United States after August 2026. Or they are held as goods in passage, which Hong Kong's figures leave out.
- By Nvidia's own account, its Hopper shipments and licensed sales to China in the two quarters to Oct 25, 2026 came to under 3.3 billion dollars [man-ms-sec-10q-2026-07] [cn-o38-nvda-q3fy27-call-transcript-pdf], and before them it had no revenue under the H200 licences [cn-o38-nvda-q1fy27-call-transcript-pdf]. At the press price that is about a fifth of what 600,000 chips would be worth.
- A large Nvidia stock in Hong Kong is plausible in itself, because Nvidia says it warehouses much of its product there.
- A reason for chips to be in Hong Kong has been reported: by the Financial Times, as Reuters relayed it on Aug 18, 2026, regulators told companies they could ship the chips to Hong Kong and use them there [cn-tolerate-reutersaug26-relay1-wayback]. That is a press report citing two unnamed people.

## What a reader may not conclude

- That the figure is confirmed, or that it is false. The months that could show it were not reached by heading here.
- Whose chips they are, whether they had been sold, or how they reached Hong Kong. No source found here says.
