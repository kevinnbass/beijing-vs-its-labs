What it shows about this claim

Alibaba told the US securities regulator that the cyberspace regulator asked Chinese technology companies to halt purchases of certain chips. Nvidia told it that the Chinese government restricted customers’ purchases, a word its later reports changed to discouraged, and that its licensed sales were restricted; the same reports say it is effectively foreclosed from the China market by US export controls. On its own results calls Nvidia said in May 2026 that it had no revenue yet from the licensed H200 chips, and in November 2026 that it had sold $2.4 billion of licensed Hopper products to China in a quarter and was no longer shipping. Alibaba also says its own chips are in scaled production. Listed Chinese firms told the Shenzhen exchange of purchases of cards named PRO 5500. One university’s notice reports buying two Nvidia cards. A tender site carries Shanghai Jiao Tong University’s preliminary procurement intention to rent six to eight servers with eight cards each. It is not yet a tender. Some central buyers’ notices of November 2026 ask for home-made computing. An award of April 2025 by the purchasing centre for central state bodies, for a university’s IT centre, names Nvidia H20 modules under a tender that allowed no imported products. None of the 37 carrier notices, filing and news page counted in point 5 names Nvidia or an Nvidia model. Some ask for home-made chips or name a software ecosystem. At least 10 of the 37 ask for neither and set no rule on origin. Two other notices name Nvidia or its cards. In 2024 a Jiangsu company that bears China Unicom’s name asked to rent computing on NV RTX 3090, NV RTX 4090 and NV Tesla A100 cards. It published its tender on China Unicom’s procurement site. The notice names no owner. In a copy on a tender site, China Unicom’s Shenzhen company seeks software services compatible with Nvidia GPUs and Ascend NPUs. The notice specifies no chip purchase or card origin. Three notices show different ways buyers set terms tied to the security-and-reliability evaluation: China Post’s Shandong branch, in purchase requirements it put out for comment, asks that the accelerator cards pass it, if results for such cards are published before the bids close; Shanghai Pudong Development Bank asks for GPU chips that meet the requirements of its category for AI training and inference chips; and a unit of China Southern Power Grid asks the AI card modules of one lot to meet the requirements of its guide. A hospital tender awards points for proof of passing the evaluation.

Not on this rung: Who gave an instruction about Nvidia chips, or how it was given. Alibaba’s sentence names the cyberspace regulator and no chip maker, and does not say how the request was made. Nvidia gave no reason for stopping. A notice to buy is not a purchase made, and a contract is not a delivery.

Read with care

  • A rung says how a statement reached us, not whether it is true. A document can be wrong and an unnamed person right. A quotation that was found in a saved copy shows what the source says.
  • The rungs follow the argument’s own labels for its 639 sources; the same source sits on one rung only. The argument has eleven kinds of source; this figure puts its state documents and what state media, industry bodies and research institutes published on rung 1, filings and buyers’ own purchase notices on rung 2, officials, official records and named people’s own statements on rung 3, and reports of named people’s words or companies’ statements, and news outlets’ accounts of documents, filings or official acts, on rung 4. Rungs 1 to 4 hold exactly the kinds the argument counts as on the record. A named person’s words on rung 4 still pass through a reporter, a post, a transcript site or a news outlet’s own transcript.
  • What the count cannot weigh: one company filing may carry more than several press reports. The count of squares is not a score.
  • English renderings of Chinese are ours, not official translations.
