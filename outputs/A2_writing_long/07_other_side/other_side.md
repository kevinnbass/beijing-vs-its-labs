## The case, in seven parts

The claim under test: Beijing's chip policy pulled against China's own AI companies, which wanted Nvidia's chips. The strongest reply is that there was a preference, not a conflict, and that part of what stood between the companies and Nvidia's chips was American.

**1.** **A vice premier and a minister welcomed Nvidia, and ministries were reported to approve purchases.** Vice Premier He Lifeng told Nvidia's chief executive on Apr 17, 2025 that Nvidia was welcome [1]. The commerce minister told him on Jul 17, 2025 that he hoped Nvidia would supply Chinese customers [2]. Reuters, Jan 30, 2026, on Yahoo Finance, from unnamed people, on the H200: "China's industry and commerce ministries have granted approvals for all four companies" (DeepSeek, ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent) [3].

**2.** **No published ban was found.** No published order against buying Nvidia chips was found in the 82 policy pages read. One of that kind was published in May 2023, on the cyberspace regulator's site, when the Cybersecurity Review Office concluded that operators of critical information infrastructure shall stop buying Micron's products [4].

**3.** **The national purchase rules stop at the state's own buying.** The purchase notices of Dec 26, 2023 require Party and government bodies at township level and above, and two kinds of public institution tied to them, to ask, when they buy desktop computers and general-purpose servers, that the processor and operating system meet a security-and-reliability evaluation; other units, the notices say, may leave that requirement out [5] [6]. No published national plan found sets a home-made share for computing centres: every such share found is a province's or a city's, and Jiangsu's plan marks its own as indicative, not binding [7].

**4.** **Nvidia still shipped AI accelerators in China, by IDC's count.** By the research firm IDC's count of China's market, "Nvidia shipped 678,000 AI accelerators in China from January through June" of 2026; the report adds that "IDC did not specify which Nvidia products made up its 34% market share" [8]. Tencent's chief strategy officer spoke on Mar 18, 2026 of buying "higher-end imported GPUs which are now becoming available again" [9].

**5.** **Washington stood in the way too.** Nvidia's reports since November 2025 say: "we are already effectively foreclosed from the China market by U.S. export controls" [10]. Washington's table of licence applications shows that by May 11, 2026 it had returned without action the applications for 55% of the chips its summary records. It gives no reason, and the House committee that released it says the summary does not show every application in full [11] [12]. The person who told Reuters that Chinese firms pulled back after guidance from Beijing also said the shift "was partly triggered by changes on the U.S. side" [13].

**6.** **Beijing may have wanted more advanced chips, not fewer.** The Congressional Research Service, Sept 19, 2025: China "may use its purported security concerns about the H20 to press for technology disclosures and access to more advanced chips, such as Nvidia's B30 chip" [14].

**7.** **The companies had a path of their own.** Alibaba says its chip unit "has brought proprietary GPUs into scaled production" [15]. Zhipu said in January 2026 that its image model had completed "its full training cycle on Chinese-made chips" [16]. The President, Donald Trump, asked on May 15, 2026 whether China had bought any H200s, spoke of China without setting its companies apart from its government: "No, because they chose not to. They want to try and develop their own." [17]

## What this case cannot explain

- **Two filings.** Nvidia told the US securities regulator on Nov 19, 2025 that the Chinese government "restricted customers from purchasing, importing, or using our data center products" [10]. Alibaba told it: "In September 2025, the Cyberspace Administration of China requested Chinese technology companies to halt purchases of certain chips." [18] That sentence does not name Nvidia.
- **Orders of which, by reports of Reuters and the Financial Times citing unnamed people, only a small part arrived.** Reuters reported in December 2025 that Chinese companies "have placed orders for more than 2 million H200 chips for 2026" [19]; in August 2026, relaying the Financial Times, that ByteDance and Tencent had each received "about 10,000 H200 processors" [20]; and in November 2026 that about 600,000 chips were stored in Hong Kong "as Beijing withholds approval for large-scale imports despite demand from technology firms" [21]. All three reports cite unnamed people.
- **Licences, and shipments that stopped.** Nvidia, on its call of May 20, 2026, of chips Washington had licensed: "we have yet to generate any revenue, and we are uncertain whether any imports will be allowed into the country" [22]. On its call of Nov 17, 2026 it reported that "Q3 sales of licensed Hopper Data Center products to China was $2.4 billion", and said: "we are no longer shipping licensed Hopper Data Center products to China" [23]; Hopper is the family of chips that includes the H200 [24].

Each part of that case is in the record, some as a reading or a company's own claim. Together they limit the claim: Beijing was not one actor; it warned in public and, by the press reports, gave unpublished guidance, and no published ban was found; some chips came in; the companies had reasons of their own and American curbs pushed too; and what Beijing meant is not settled. They do not show that nothing stood between the companies and the chips they were reported to order.
