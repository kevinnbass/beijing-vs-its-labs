The answer so far

  • Yes on direction, with five limits. From 2024 to 2026 the Chinese state’s declared policy, and what its regulators did, pulled China’s AI companies away from Nvidia chips; the companies went on using those chips, ordering them and asking for them. The limits: Beijing was not one actor; the pressure came as public warnings and unpublished guidance, not as a published ban; some purchases were approved and some chips came in; the companies had reasons of their own, and American curbs and conditions pushed too; and what Beijing meant by it is not settled. That is the project’s argument as rebuilt on Nov 23, 2026, in short; the records below are its dated evidence.

What would change the answer next

  • Nvidia’s 10-Q for the quarter ended Oct 25, 2026, and what it says of the Chinese government. The wording has changed from report to report: in November 2025 the government “restricted customers from purchasing, importing, or using our data center products” (Nvidia's 10-Q, November 2025); in the report of August 2026 the sales under the American licences “were restricted by the PRC government” (Nvidia's 10-Q, August 2026). Whether the next report keeps, drops or adds to those words is the next record on Nvidia’s own side.
  • Any published Chinese rule. No published Chinese order against buying Nvidia chips was found in the 103 policy pages read, and such an order could have been found: the 2023 review conclusion telling operators of critical information infrastructure to stop buying Micron’s products is among those pages, “应停止采购美光公司产品” (the 2023 review conclusion). A published order, or a published permit rule for these imports, would change the second limit: so far the pressure is public warnings and unpublished guidance.
  • The end of the antitrust case. No page read says the Nvidia case ended: the market regulator’s review of its 2025 work, posted Apr 10, 2026, still lists “对英伟达公司实施进一步调查” (a further investigation of Nvidia) (the regulator's review of 2025), and its news list to Nov 23, 2026 has a suspension of the case against DuPont China, “市场监管总局暂停对杜邦中国集团有限公司的反垄断调查程序”, and none for Nvidia (the regulator's news list; the same list, newest page). By Nvidia’s account the preliminary finding is that “our compliance with applicable U.S. export controls, which required us to offer degraded products to the Chinese market, discriminated unfairly against customers in the China market” (Nvidia's 10-Q, August 2026). An end to the case would be a new act of the state; it would not by itself settle what Beijing meant, the fifth limit.

Read with care

  • Open marks rest on unnamed people. What they say Beijing told companies is reported, not published policy.
  • No published Chinese order against buying Nvidia chips was found in the 103 policy pages read. The published acts against Nvidia that were found are a summons and an antitrust case.
  • The records are a selection made to show both sides, not a census. The number of records on a side is not a measure.
  • A quotation that is found shows what the source says, not that it is true.
  • The state of play is written on its date from the saved sources; it does not update itself. Its counts of the Commerce Department’s table are a script’s sums of the table’s rows, and they add Nvidia’s and AMD’s chips together.
  • Caption tracks and machine transcripts have not been checked by ear; where one is quoted, the time in the recording is given.
  • English in round brackets after Chinese is our rendering, not an official translation.
  • A script looked for each record’s date in the readable text of its saved page. It found 54 in full and 3 by month and day, with the year elsewhere in the page. 2 records carry a label instead of a day. It did not find Apr 22, 2026 (Howard Lutnick, Senate hearing (Rev transcript)); Nov 5, 2026 (Reuters (its dateline is Nov 5), on CNA, whose page is dated Nov 6). Check such a date against the page before quoting it.
