# Who else says it, and who narrows it

*Officials, companies, congressional bodies and analysts on whether Beijing held back chips that China's own AI companies wanted, and on what Beijing meant by it. Each row gives their own words, the date and the source. A statement that the conflict exists is not proof of it.*

Draft by Claude, Anthropic's model. Written Nov 23, 2026 (UTC).

Commentary by analysts is marked as opinion. It is not evidence of fact. English in round brackets after Chinese is ours, not an official translation.

Words taken from captions or a machine transcript are marked so, with the time in the recording. Nobody has checked them by ear.

What a body is comes from a saved page, or is marked (general knowledge) where no saved page was used for it.

## What this page is for

Palmer Luckey said in a clip that China does not want Nvidia to sell chips to Chinese companies, while Chinese companies want to buy them. This page lists who else has said the same, who has said it only in part, who has said something that narrows or cuts against it, and how American bodies read what Beijing meant by it.

Four things to keep in mind. The Commerce Secretary, the Trade Representative and the Treasury Secretary each give theirs as their own understanding, sense, analysis or view; the President states his flatly. No published instruction was found in the 82 policy pages read, and Beijing's spokespeople would not confirm one. American bodies read Beijing's conduct mostly two ways, as leverage in trade talks and as a sovereign choice, and the argument leaves that open (its fifth limit). And the analysts give opinions: they are sorted here by what they say, not by whether they are right.

## 4. What Beijing meant: leverage, or a sovereign choice

The argument's fifth limit: what Beijing meant by its acts is not settled. No Chinese document found states a motive for the unpublished guidance to companies, or ties an act against Nvidia to the trade talks. Beijing's own texts give purposes for its public acts: the cyberspace regulator summoned Nvidia "为维护中国用户网络安全、数据安全" (to safeguard Chinese users' cyber and data security) [42]; an unsigned article on the work of the market regulator's Party group says the regulator's cases against Nvidia, Qualcomm and DuPont China "有力维护国家利益" (forcefully safeguarded national interests) [43]; and the regulator's annual report for 2025 lists the further investigation of Nvidia among cases that served "维护公平竞争" (to protect fair competition) [44].

The American bodies and officials below read Beijing's conduct, mostly in two ways: as leverage in the trade talks, or as a sovereign choice. The Treasury Secretary gave a third reading. None of them quotes a Chinese text that states a motive for the unpublished guidance.

| Who and when | Their own words | How they read it |
|---|---|---|
| **US-China Economic and Security Review Commission**, report of November 2025 [45] | The market regulator's finding against Nvidia came "in the midst of negotiations", as the regulator dropped an inquiry into Google, "signaling China's willingness to both exert leverage and ease pressure as a negotiating tactic" | Leverage. A congressional commission's report; this passage rests on Reuters and the Financial Times. |
| **The White House**, fact sheet on the trade deal, Nov 1, 2025 [46] | "China will terminate its various investigations targeting U.S. companies in the semiconductor supply chain, including its antitrust, anti-monopoly, and anti-dumping investigations"; among China's commitments: "End Chinese retaliation against U.S. semiconductor manufacturers and other major U.S. companies." | Leverage, or retaliation. No page read says the Nvidia case ended. |
| **The Trade Representative's office**, report of March 2026 [47] | By the end of 2025 China "had terminated or suspended these investigations", which "were being pursued for retaliatory purposes" | Retaliation. The passage names no company. |

A research service's reading for Congress. |
| **Senator Tim Kaine**, on the Senate floor, Dec 18, 2025 [49] | The administration had first claimed it would permit only "inferior chips, such as the Nvidia H20, not the H200", "but China declined. ## 7. What Luckey said in the same interview, in a published transcript

The clip is 56 seconds of a longer interview. A published transcript of the interview has two sentences that bear on how to read him; the second is also in the clip [68].

- His own view of the policy: "I don't think that we should be exporting chips to China." [68]
- His reason for the 600,000 chips: "because China will not give them an import permit because they want to build an internal domestic processor industry" [68]. The clip's machine transcript has the same words at 0:25 to 0:35 [17]; it has not been checked by ear. His words say more than the record shows. No permit rule for these chips was found in the policy pages read or in China's lists of goods that need an import licence for 2024, 2025 and 2026 (the 2026 list: [69]), and the words of the Reuters report whose figure of 600,000 matches his are that Beijing "withholds approval for large-scale imports" [70].

## Read with care

- **Saying it is not showing it.** The American officials in section 1 give their understanding, sense or analysis. What shows the conflict is in the argument: the documents, the filings and the reports.
- **The Chinese side has not confirmed it.** No published instruction against buying Nvidia chips was found in the 82 policy pages read, and the foreign ministry's spokespeople gave no answer either way.
- **Washington stood in the way too.** The House committee's table shows that the applications for 55% of the chips the summary records, Nvidia's H200 and AMD's MI325X, were returned without action, with no reason given; the American rule and a 25% tariff also bore on the H200 (section 3, and the argument's point 20).
- **What Beijing meant is read more than one way.** Section 4 sets the readings side by side. They are readings, not findings.
- **Captions and clips.** Words from captions and machine transcripts are marked, with the time; nobody has checked them by ear. Both clips also make claims about Nvidia's chief executive that are outside this page. Those are the speakers' allegations, and no saved source here establishes them.
- **Commentary is opinion.** An analyst who states the conflict adds no evidence that it exists.
- **Not here.** Press reports from unnamed people that state the conflict, such as Reuters's headline "Chinese firms still want Nvidia chips despite government pressure not to buy, sources say" [71], are in the argument and in TIER2_REPORTING.md.
